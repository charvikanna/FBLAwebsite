Lesson 1: Introduction to Financial Statements

Understanding the three core financial statements.

1 / 4: Content

1. Income Statement

The Income Statement (also called a Profit and Loss statement) shows what a business earned and spent over a period of time, such as a month, quarter, or year. It helps answer a simple question: "Did this business make money after covering its costs?" The basic flow is Revenue → COGS → Gross Profit → Operating Expenses → Net Income.

An income statement is one of the primary financial statements an entity prepares. It reflects the entity's financial performance over a specific period of time, such as the month of June.

The income statement includes the following financial components of an entity's business:

  • Revenue: the value of the products or services sold
  • Expenses: the value of expenses incurred, such as labor costs, rent, interest expense, or tax expense
  • Net Income (Loss): the amount of profit the entity made or loss it incurred after subtracting all its expenses from its revenue

Did You Know?

The net income or net loss (the "bottom line") reported on the income statement moves into the retained earnings/accumulated loss account on the balance sheet at the end of each month. Net income increases retained earnings (or reduces accumulated loss). Net loss reduces retained earnings (or increases accumulated loss).

The income statement equation: Revenue - Expenses = Net Income (Loss)

Summary: The income statement shows whether operations created profit during a period by comparing revenue against all related expenses.

2. Balance Sheet

A balance sheet is one of the primary financial statements an entity prepares. It reflects the company's financial condition at a specific point in time, for instance as of June 30.

The balance sheet includes the following financial components of an entity's business:

  • Assets: what the entity owns
  • Liabilities: what the entity owes
  • Equity: the difference between assets and liabilities, or residual interest

Did You Know?

The balance sheet gets its name because total assets must equal the total of liabilities and equity, which is why it always balances.

Fundamental accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Equity

Summary: The balance sheet captures what a company owns, what it owes, and what is left for owners at a single point in time.

3. Cash Flow Statement

Cash flow is the net balance of cash that comes into and goes out of a business over a period. Cash flows in when customers pay and flows out when the business pays suppliers, payroll, rent, utilities, taxes, and other obligations.

Even profitable businesses can run into trouble when payments are delayed by credit terms or financing arrangements. That is why cash flow analysis is essential for survival, planning, valuation, and lender/investor reporting.

Why Cash Flow Matters

  • Liquidity: Positive cash flow means more cash is coming in than going out during the period.
  • Risk management: Negative cash flow can create shortfalls that threaten operations.
  • Decision support: Historical and forecast cash flow helps plan expansion, investments, and financing needs.
  • Valuation and compliance: Cash flow is a major indicator of financial health and is commonly required in loan and investor reporting.

Structure of a Cash Flow Statement

Most formats include these three sections:

  • Cash Flow from Operations: core business inflows (customer receipts, interest income) and outflows (suppliers, salaries, taxes, operating expenses).
  • Cash Flow from Investing: buying/selling long-term assets and investments (for example, equipment purchases or proceeds from asset sales).
  • Cash Flow from Financing: debt and equity cash flows (borrowing, issuing shares, repayments, dividends, buybacks).

Direct vs. Indirect Method

  • Direct method: lists actual cash receipts and cash payments during the period.
  • Indirect method: starts with net income, then adjusts for non-cash items and working-capital changes (accounts receivable, accounts payable, inventory).

Strategies to Improve Cash Flow

  • Run customer credit checks and tighten payment terms when needed.
  • Invoice immediately and encourage early payment discounts.
  • Improve inventory turnover and reduce slow-moving stock.
  • Negotiate supplier terms, consider leasing, and use lines of credit strategically.
  • Review pricing and payment systems to speed up collections.

Summary: Cash flow statements show whether a business has the timing and amount of cash needed to operate, invest, and grow safely.
