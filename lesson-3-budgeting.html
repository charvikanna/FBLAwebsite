Lesson 3: Budgeting & Saving

50/30/20 rule and emergency funds.

1 / 4: Content

What Is a Budget?

A budget is a money plan: it shows how much comes in, how much goes out, and how much is left for savings or goals. A strong budget helps prevent overspending, reduces debt risk, and gives you clearer control over decisions.

Why it matters: money is limited, so budgeting helps you prioritize what is most important now while still planning for future needs. It also helps separate emotional spending from intentional spending.

Track Cash Flow

Use net income (after taxes) as your baseline, then categorize every expense weekly. This reveals patterns like subscriptions, convenience spending, or seasonal spikes that are hard to notice day-to-day.

Reach Goals

Convert goals into dollar targets and deadlines. For example, a $1,200 goal in 12 months requires about $100/month, which turns a vague goal into a concrete plan.

Control Debt

Budgeting creates room for on-time payments and lowers the chance of late fees. Over time, steady repayment protects credit history and reduces total interest paid.

Build Stability

Planned bills and emergency savings reduce financial stress. The goal is to avoid a single surprise expense causing long-term debt or missed obligations.

The 50/30/20 Rule

Use your monthly take-home pay to split spending into Needs, Wants, and Savings.

This rule is a practical starting framework, not a strict law. In expensive cities, needs may exceed 50%, so the adjustment usually comes from reducing wants temporarily while income grows.

Needs 50% Wants 30% Savings 20%
Category Formula On $2,000 Income Examples
Needs Income x 0.50 $1,000 Rent, groceries, transport, utilities, insurance
Wants Income x 0.30 $600 Eating out, hobbies, entertainment, travel
Savings Income x 0.20 $400 Emergency fund, college fund, investing

Step-by-Step Use

1) Know income, 2) calculate targets, 3) sort spending, 4) compare against targets monthly.

When Income Varies

Use the lowest expected month for needs planning and treat extra months as savings catch-up.

Quick method: calculate targets first, then map real transactions from your bank app into each bucket.

How to Build and Balance a Budget

A budget is only useful if it gets updated with real spending. Most people don’t fail because they chose the wrong formula; they fail because they stop reviewing transactions and miss drift.

Step Action
1List monthly net income from all sources.
2Calculate 50/30/20 targets for needs, wants, savings.
3Record actual spending by category from statements.
4Compare actual spending vs targets and find gaps.
5Shift extra funds temporarily to cover negatives.
6Fix root causes: reduce variable expenses or increase income.
7Repeat monthly and automate transfers to savings first.

Increase Income

Ask for a raise/hours, take side work, sell unused items, and redirect gifts to savings goals. Even temporary income boosts can close short-term budget gaps quickly.

Decrease Expenses

Target variable costs first: compare prices, avoid impulse buys, meal prep, and use lower-cost activities. Fixed costs usually need negotiation or contract changes and take longer.

If one category goes negative, shifting funds from a positive category is a short-term fix; long-term balance comes from changing habits or income.

Per Unit Pricing: Find the Better Deal

Unit pricing compares cost per item/ounce/pound and can reveal better value even when sticker prices look similar.

Stores often print unit price on shelf labels in small text. If not shown, calculate it manually: unit cost = total price / quantity. This works for groceries, school supplies, and household items.

Emergency Fund and Unplanned Expenses

Planned expenses are expected bills; unplanned expenses are surprises like repairs or medical costs. An emergency fund protects you from going into debt when these happen.

Without emergency savings, an unexpected bill often leads to high-interest borrowing. That creates a cycle where future income goes to debt payments instead of goals.

Target: save 3-6 months of essential expenses (not discretionary wants).

Ella Example Amount
Monthly essential expenses$2,600
3-month emergency target$7,800
6-month emergency target$15,600
Monthly savings (20% of $5,200)$1,040

Best practice: build in stages (starter fund -> 1 month -> 3 months -> 6 months) so progress is measurable and motivating.

Saving Accounts and Interest Growth

Savings methods matter because where you keep money affects growth, access, and risk. Cash in a jar does not grow; interest-bearing accounts do.

Interest is a percentage paid on your balance. Compound interest means each period’s interest is added to principal, so future interest is earned on a larger base.

How to Choose an Account

Check your goal, minimum deposit requirements, withdrawal limits, and fees before choosing where to save.

Practical Strategy

Keep emergency money in a liquid high-yield savings account and use term products like CDs only for money you will not need soon.

Short summary: save consistently, protect emergency liquidity, and let compound growth work over time.
