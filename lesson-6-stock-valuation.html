Lesson 6: Stock Valuation Methods

DCF, comparable analysis, and dividend discount models.

1 / 4: Content

1. Discounted Cash Flow (DCF)

DCF estimates what a business is worth today based on the cash it may generate in the future. Because future money is less certain and less valuable than money today, those future cash flows are discounted back to present value. The sum of those discounted values gives an estimate of intrinsic value.

In real life, DCF is less about perfect prediction and more about disciplined assumptions: growth rate, margins, reinvestment, and risk. Small assumption changes can move valuation a lot, so always test multiple scenarios (base, optimistic, conservative).

2. Comparable Analysis

Comparable analysis values a company by looking at similar firms and their market multiples such as P/E or EV/EBITDA. It asks: \"How is the market pricing similar businesses right now?\" This method is useful because it reflects current market sentiment and industry context.

The key is choosing true comparables: similar business model, size, growth profile, and risk. Bad peer selection can create misleading conclusions.

3. Dividend Discount Model (DDM)

DDM is designed for companies that pay stable dividends. A common simplified form is Value = D / (r - g), where D is next dividend, r is required return, and g is expected dividend growth. This model works best when dividend policy is predictable.

Insurance and utility companies are often used in dividend-based analysis because cash generation and payouts can be steadier than high-growth startups. For firms without reliable dividends, DCF or comparables are usually better tools.

Daily-life takeaway: Do not rely on one formula. Good decision-making compares methods, checks assumptions, and stays alert for fraud red flags (unrealistic guaranteed returns, pressure to act quickly, or unverifiable financial claims).
